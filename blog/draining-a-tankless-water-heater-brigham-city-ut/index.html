How to Drain and Flush a Tankless Water Heater in Brigham City, UT

Draining a tankless water heater in Brigham City, UT really means flushing the heat exchanger with a mild acid, and most owners with service valves can do it in an afternoon.

By Brigham City Pro Plumbing. Published October 29, 2026. Updated October 29, 2026. 6 min read.

The short answer before you pick up a hose

A tankless unit has no big tank to empty. What people call draining is a flush: you run a descaling liquid, usually white vinegar or a product your manual approves, through the heat exchanger in a closed loop. That dissolves the mineral crust that builds on the metal and chokes flow.

Yes, you can do it yourself if the unit already has two service valves on the water lines. If it does not, a plumber needs to add them first, because forcing a flush without isolation valves means cutting into live pipe.

Brigham City's own water page reports hardness in the high teens of grains per gallon. That is a lot of dissolved mineral for a heat exchanger to cope with, so expect to flush on the short end of whatever interval your manual gives.

The flush, one step at a time

  1. Cut the power and the gas

    Unplug the unit or switch off its breaker, then close the gas shutoff. If you ever smell gas, leave the house first, then call 911 or the gas utility from a safe distance.

  2. Close the water and open the relief ports

    Shut the cold supply and the hot outlet at the service valves. Open the small purge caps on each valve to release pressure. Expect a little hot water, so wear gloves.

  3. Build the loop

    Connect one hose from the pump to the cold side valve and another from the hot side valve back to the bucket. Pour in the descaling liquid so the pump can circulate it.

  4. Run the pump

    Turn the pump on and let the liquid circulate for the time your manual sets. Cloudy or tinted liquid tells you scale is coming loose.

  5. Rinse and clear the screen

    Drain the bucket, then flush with clean water for several minutes. Pull the cold inlet filter screen, rinse off grit, and reinstall it.

  6. Restore everything and test

    Close the purge caps, reset the valves to open, restore gas and power, and run a hot tap. Watch every fitting for drips for the first few minutes.

How often the flush should happen

Manuals usually set an interval in months, and it is written for average water. Hard supply shortens it. A simple habit works well: write the flush date on the unit with a marker, and check the inlet screen halfway to the next flush.

Watch the hot water itself. If the time it takes to reach a steady temperature grows, or the flow at a far bathroom drops while cold flow stays strong, scale is likely building. Flush early rather than waiting for an error code, because a heavily coated exchanger is harder to clear and can overheat.

Keep a short log of each flush: date, solution used, how cloudy the liquid looked, and any code that showed up. A technician will find that record useful, and it helps you spot a slow decline before the unit fails.

Signs the descaling is not enough

  • Hot water cuts in and out even after a clean flush and a clean screen.
  • The display keeps showing an error code that returns within days.
  • The vinegar comes out of the unit with almost no color, yet flow is still weak.
  • You hear popping or rumbling that did not exist before.
  • The unit is old and the manual says exchanger replacement is the next step.

Any one of these points to something beyond scale, such as a failing sensor, a damaged exchanger, or a gas supply problem. A repeat flush will not fix those, and a technician with a manometer and the model's service sheet can find the cause faster.

Mistakes that turn a flush into a repair

Skipping the gas shutoff

A flush with the gas supply still open can fire the burner on a dry exchanger. Close it every time.

Using the wrong liquid

Strong acids or bleach can attack seals and metal. Stick to what the manual lists.

Forgetting the screen

A clogged inlet screen mimics scale. Clean it during every flush.

Leaving valves in the service position

If the valves are not reopened, the unit starves and trips an error. Check handle direction before you walk away.

When to hand the job to a licensed plumber

Stop if the unit has no service valves, if a fitting will not turn without force, or if you find a leak you cannot trace. Also stop if the gas line looks corroded or the vent pipe is loose.

A tankless water heater technician can add valves, flush the unit, and test the combustion setup in one visit. If the unit needs to come out entirely, a plan for water heater replacement should cover venting and gas sizing as well as the swap itself.
