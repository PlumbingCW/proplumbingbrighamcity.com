Tankless Water Heater Size Guide for Brigham City, UT

Sizing a tankless water heater in Brigham City, UT takes two numbers, the flow you need at peak and how much the water must be heated.

By Brigham City Pro Plumbing. Published October 29, 2026. Updated October 29, 2026. 7 min read.

Two numbers decide the size

A tankless heater is sized by flow rate in gallons per minute (GPM) and temperature rise, the number of degrees it must add to incoming water. Add up the fixtures you run at the same moment, then subtract your coldest incoming water temperature from the hot temperature you want.

Pick a unit whose rated output at that temperature rise covers your peak flow. A heater that looks big on the box can fall short in January, because every model delivers less flow as the rise grows.

Step one: add up what runs at the same time

Typical flow by fixture. Check your own fixtures' labels for exact figures.
FixtureTypical flow (GPM)Likely to run at once?
ShowerAbout 2.0 to 2.5Yes, often two at once on busy mornings.
Bathroom faucetAbout 1.0 to 1.5Sometimes.
Kitchen faucetAbout 1.5 to 2.2Often, during a meal.
DishwasherAbout 1.0 to 1.5At night, with a shower.
Washing machineAbout 1.5 to 3.0Weekends, when everyone is home.

Count the busiest realistic moment, not every fixture in the house. A family of five with two showers and a dishwasher running has a very different peak than a couple.

Step two: find the temperature rise

Incoming water is coldest in winter, and Brigham City's January low averages 18.1 F. Size for that month, not July. Your plumber can tell you the real incoming temperature for your line, or measure it.

Here is a made-up example to show the math, not a recommendation. If incoming water is 40 F and you want 120 F at the tap, the rise is 80 degrees. Two showers at 2.5 GPM plus a 1.5 GPM kitchen faucet need 6.5 GPM, and the unit must deliver that at an 80 degree rise.

Reading a spec sheet without getting lost

  • Rated flow at a stated temperature rise. Compare units at your rise, not the highest number on the box.
  • Maximum gas input, which tells your plumber what the gas line must supply.
  • Minimum flow to fire, since a very slow trickle at a tap may not turn the burner on.
  • Venting type and clearances, which decide where the unit can hang.
  • Service access for flushing the heat exchanger.

If a spec sheet leaves out the flow at a given rise, ask for it. A unit that cannot show you that number is hard to size honestly.

Where sizing meets your gas line or panel

A larger tankless heater burns more gas per minute, and the gas piping to it must be big enough to supply it. That is a plumber's check, not a guess. An older house may need a larger gas line.

The vent matters as well. Many tankless units use special venting, and it must be installed to the maker's directions. Electric models draw heavy current and need panel capacity, which means a licensed electrician.

Hard water changes the long-term picture

Scale coats the heat exchanger and slows heat transfer, so a unit that was sized well on day one loses margin as it ages. Flushing the unit on a schedule keeps scale from building. A water softener upstream reduces the scale that forms in the first place.

Ask for a unit whose manual makes flushing easy, with service valves you can reach. Skipping flushes is the quiet reason many tankless units fail early.

Sizing mistakes that cost comfort

Average instead of peak

The heater has to meet the peak, not the average. Peak is the 7 a.m. rush.

Summer numbers in a winter town

Incoming water is much colder in January. A unit sized for July runs lukewarm in winter.

Bigger is not always better

A larger unit needs more gas supply and venting, which can add work. The right size is the smallest that meets your peak.

Long pipe runs overlooked

Water that must travel far to a fixture wastes time. A recirculation setup or a second unit may suit a large house.

Questions to bring to a pro

  1. What is my peak flow, and how did you count it?
  2. What is my real incoming water temperature in January?
  3. Is my existing gas line big enough, and what would change?
  4. Where does it vent, and who has verified the route?
  5. How do I flush it, and where are the service valves?

If you ever smell gas

Tankless units are gas appliances. If you smell gas anywhere in the house, leave immediately, then call 911 or your gas utility from outside. Do not try to find the source yourself.

Enbridge Gas Utah lists a 24-hour emergency line at 1-800-767-1689. Save it in your phone now, and tell everyone in the house what the plan is. Any gas work behind a new heater, including the line sizing, belongs to a licensed professional.

Local note

Brigham City note: winter lows and hard water

NOAA climate normals show Brigham City's average January low at 18.1 F, and the city sees about 38.3 inches of snow a year. Cold winters mean colder incoming water, so size the heater for January, not July, or showers will run lukewarm exactly when you need them hot.

The Brigham City Water Division lists hardness at 17 to 20 grains per gallon. That scale builds on a tankless heat exchanger and cuts into performance. Plan for flushing, and ask Public Works for the current hardness figure before you choose a unit.
