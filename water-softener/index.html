What the city's own page says about your water

A water softener is a treatment tank that removes hardness minerals, mostly calcium and magnesium, from the water entering your home. Brigham City's water page states hardness of 17 to 20 grains per gallon, and the USGS scale calls anything over 10.5 very hard. By that yardstick, the city's water sits well inside the very hard band.

The city draws from 17 sources in its 2025 report, 8 springs and 9 wells, so the figure can drift a little by season and by tap. Ask Public Works for the current number before you size a unit, because the number sets how often the softener must regenerate.

What very hard water leaves behind at home

Spotted glasses and fixtures

White film dries on glass, chrome and shower doors no matter how often you wipe.

Clogged aerators and showerheads

Chalky crust narrows the holes, so flow weakens and sprays veer sideways.

Worn faucet cartridges

Grit scratches ceramic discs and rubber seals, which leads to drips.

Dull laundry and dry skin

Soap reacts with minerals, so you use more of it and rinse less cleanly.

Scaled appliances

Dishwashers, ice makers and coffee machines collect crust that shortens their lives.

The hardest-working appliance is the heater. Scale insulates the elements and the burner floor, which is why our water heater repair crews see so many scale failures, and why a tankless water heater depends on treated water to keep its exchanger open.

Softener, conditioner, filter or reverse osmosis: which does what

Water treatment choices compared.
DeviceWhat it doesWhat it does not do
Salt-based softenerRemoves hardness through ion exchangeDoes not filter sediment, chlorine or taste.
Salt-free conditionerChanges how minerals stick to surfacesDoes not remove hardness minerals from the water.
Whole-house carbon filterReduces chlorine taste and odorDoes not soften.
Under-sink reverse osmosisTreats drinking and cooking water at one tapDoes not protect pipes or appliances.
Sediment filterCatches grit and rust particlesDoes not touch dissolved minerals.

Brigham City's chlorine residual averages 0.44 ppm, and lead is reported as non-detect in the 2025 report. If taste is your complaint, add a carbon filter. If scale is the complaint, the softener is the tool.

Where a softener goes and how it is installed

  1. Choose the spot

    Near the main water entry, with a drain nearby, a power outlet and room for a salt tank.

  2. Add a bypass

    A bypass valve lets you isolate the unit for service without shutting off the house.

  3. Plumb in and out

    Hard water goes in, soft water comes out, and outdoor spigots can stay on hard water if you prefer.

  4. Run the drain and brine line

    The regeneration cycle needs a legal drain connection and an air gap.

  5. Program and test

    We set hardness, capacity and cycle time, then regenerate and test.

Softener installs touch the supply and drain, so plumbing installation standards apply. Permit rules vary, so ask the Brigham City building office at (435) 734-6604 before starting.

Repairing or reinstalling an existing unit

Many calls are about a unit that already exists: a broken control valve after a move, a cut regeneration line during a remodel, a mushed salt tank or a unit that has stopped regenerating. We look at the valve, the injector, the brine line and the drain.

Some brands need specific parts or software. Tell dispatch the model on the label, and we will say honestly whether it is something we can service or whether swapping the head is the better value.

Wells, private supplies and drinking-water add-ons

Homes on a private well need a water test first, because iron, sulfur and sediment change what treatment is required. A softener does not cure every well-water problem. Ask a certified lab to test before spending money.

For drinking water only, an under-sink reverse osmosis system at the kitchen tap is a smaller project. It is not a substitute for protecting pipes and heaters, but it does handle taste.

Salt, upkeep and what to expect month to month

A salt-based unit needs salt added every few weeks, depending on use. Water feels slippery, soap lathers well and spots fade within a week or two. Check the brine tank for bridging, a hard crust that stops salt from dissolving.

Plan on a yearly look at the valve and resin. If water suddenly feels hard again, the usual causes are empty salt, a clogged injector or a stuck valve. A dripping valve or a leaking tank is a plumbing call, and dispatch is 24/7 for that. Fixture wear that comes from long exposure may be fixed with a faucet repair visit.

New unit or service

Installing a purchased unit costs less than a full system.

Extras

Access
