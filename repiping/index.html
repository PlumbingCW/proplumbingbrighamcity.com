What a repipe replaces, and what it leaves alone

Repiping in Brigham City, UT means taking out the pressurized water lines that feed your faucets, fixtures and water heater, then running new ones. It covers hot and cold supply pipe, not the drains that carry waste away.

Some homes need every line replaced. Others only need one bad run, such as the hot side or a single bathroom branch. A good plan starts by finding out which of those you really have.

Symptoms

Signs the old lines are wearing out

People describe the same handful of problems before a repipe comes up. If several of these sound familiar, the pipes are worth a closer look.

  • Brown or rust-colored water when you first open a tap in the morning, especially on the hot side.
  • Pressure that keeps dropping at showers and sinks even though the street supply seems fine.
  • Pinhole leaks that show up as damp drywall, stained ceilings or bubbling paint, then come back in a new spot after a patch.
  • Green or white crust on joints and fittings, or flaking, scaly pipe where a line enters a wall.
  • A water bill creeping up with no change in how many people live in the house.
  • Several repairs on the same plumbing in a couple of years, each one on a different stretch of pipe.

Finding out what is inside your walls

Older homes near downtown Main Street may still have galvanized steel or older copper lines, but the age of a house does not tell you what is in it. Past owners may have swapped sections. Nobody should quote a whole-house job without looking first.

We start by tracing the pipe from the water heater and the main shutoff, checking visible runs in the basement, crawlspace or utility room. A pressure test shows how well the system holds with fixtures off. Where a wall hides a suspect run, we may open a small inspection hole instead of guessing.

If you only suspect a slow leak, a leak detection visit can pinpoint it before you commit to anything larger.

Repair one section or replace the whole system

Patching is the right call when the damage is isolated and the rest of the pipe is sound. It stops making sense when failures keep moving from place to place. This table shows how the options line up.

Yard work

The water service line: meter or curb to your house

The service line is the single pipe that brings water from the street connection to your home. Callers describe it as a soggy patch of lawn, water coming up through the ground, unusually thick weeds along one strip, or no water inside at all.

Residents may not open the city water meter. Public Works at (435) 734-6615 handles anything on the meter side, so ask them where the city's responsibility ends and yours begins before any digging starts.

Any trench needs a utility locate first. Call 811 (or 800-662-4111) at least 3 business days ahead and no more than 14 calendar days before you dig, so buried gas, power and communication lines get marked. Replacement then runs from the curb or meter setting to the house, with a new shutoff where the line enters the building.

Low water pressure that keeps getting worse

Weak flow at a shower or two sinks has several possible causes, and old pipe is only one. Mineral buildup or corrosion narrows the inside of a line over the years, so water squeezes through a smaller opening.

Before blaming the pipes, check whether the weak flow hits one fixture or all of them. One fixture usually means a clogged aerator or cartridge. Every fixture, hot and cold, points toward the service line, a partly closed main valve or a pressure problem on the street side. The city offers water pressure testing, which helps rule the supply in or out.

Weak pressure on hot water only suggests the water heater or its lines, not the whole house.

What moves the price of a repipe

Size of the house

More fixtures and longer runs mean more pipe and more hours.

Wall and ceiling access

Open basements and crawlspaces are quicker than finished walls.

Pipe material chosen

Different materials cost different amounts and behave differently under heat and pressure.

Drywall and finish repair

Opened walls need patching, and painting may be a separate trade.

Yard length and ground

A service line job depends on how far the trench runs and what it crosses.

Homeowners policies often cover sudden accidental water damage, but they usually do not pay for worn-out pipe or the failed part itself. Read your policy or ask your agent before assuming a repipe is covered.

How a repipe job runs, start to finish

  1. Walk-through

    We trace the lines, test pressure and photograph what we find so you can see it too.

  2. Permit check

    Permit rules vary, so ask the Brigham City building office at (435) 734-6604 before starting.

  3. Water off and rough-in

    Water is shut off, new lines are run, and most homes stay without water for part of a day.

  4. Pressure test and restore

    New runs are tested under pressure, then reconnected to fixtures and the water heater.

  5. Wall repair plan

    Openings are left ready for drywall repair, which you or a separate trade will finish.
